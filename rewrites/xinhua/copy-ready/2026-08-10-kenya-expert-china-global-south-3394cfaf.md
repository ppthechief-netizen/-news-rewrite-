10th August 2026 - (Nairobi) Kenyan research policy analyst Lewis Ndichu has rejected so-called "China squeeze" claims, arguing that Chinese manufacturing support is helping Global South countries industrialise rather than crowding them out.

In a written interview, Ndichu, of the Nairobi-based Africa Policy Institute, said the squeeze narrative was deeply misleading. He argued it flattens complex trade and investment ties into a zero-sum contest and wrongly assumes that China's rise must come at other developing countries' expense.

"China has become a major trading partner, source of investment," he said, describing Beijing as an infrastructure developer and technology partner across much of the Global South. Engagement, he added, has moved beyond buying raw materials toward connectivity, manufacturing and industrial capacity.

Ndichu said Chinese manufacturing had helped lower the cost of industrialisation for many African economies by tackling the basics first. For years China has taken part in Kenya's railways, roads, ports and energy projects, easing logistics bottlenecks and tying the country more tightly into regional markets. Better links, he said, cut cargo costs, drew investment, strengthened supply chains and helped position Kenya as a regional production and logistics hub.

As infrastructure improved, Chinese firms also raised manufacturing investment in Kenya, spanning building materials, agricultural processing, electric mobility and renewable energy. Those projects, Ndichu said, were creating jobs, deepening local supply chains and bringing in new technologies and skills — a catalyst for domestic firms and regional value chains as Kenya shifts from a finished-goods market toward production, value addition and learning.

He argued that foreign investment should be judged not only by capital size but by jobs, supplier development, skills and technology transfer, and whether production embeds in the local economy. In electric mobility, Chinese investment had helped electric buses, motorcycles, batteries and related services form a new industrial ecosystem. "The deeper value, however, lies not simply in the capital," he noted, pointing to lasting capabilities.

Kenya already draws about 90 per cent of its electricity from renewable sources such as geothermal, hydro, wind and solar. Ndichu said Chinese green technologies — solar photovoltaics, batteries, power electronics and electric mobility — had lowered cost and technology barriers, with the significance shifting from importing kit to embedding green technology in Kenya's productive economy.

Since 1st May, China has fully implemented zero-tariff treatment for 53 African countries with which it has diplomatic relations. Ndichu said that should cut costs for avocados, coffee, chillies and apples entering China and could spur investment in processing, cold chains, packaging and logistics, helping Africa move up from primary commodities. He contrasted China's focus on infrastructure, productive capacity and partnership with Western aid models often tied to wider governance conditions, saying Beijing's emphasis on sovereignty, non-interference and practical outcomes appeals across the Global South.
