10th August 2026 - (Hong Kong) As artificial intelligence spreads through offices and classrooms, Professor Kar Yan Tam, vice-president for administration and business at the Hong Kong University of Science and Technology, said 44 per cent of respondents reported higher efficiency without a change to their core duties, while 12 per cent said their roles had widened to reviewing, supervising and correcting AI output — a sign that human-machine collaboration is becoming a working model of its own.

HKUST today invited three early-career women from education, retail and telecom to describe how they use the tools. Chan Oi-wai, who is about to become a secondary biology teacher, said AI is not yet common in school placements, yet she already treats it as a lesson-planning partner for slides, resource packs and research so she can spend more time on design and knowing her pupils.

She also brainstorms with chatbots to test whether a lesson meets curriculum goals, sparks interest and runs smoothly. Even so, she does not fear teachers being replaced: the job is more than transmitting facts. Some pupils have already pasted AI-generated homework that proved wrong or invented, she said, so educators must teach responsible use, independent thought, fact-checking, critical reading and joining knowledge across subjects.

Luxury retail customer manager Lam Chiu-kei said her group rolled out "MalA", an in-house AI platform that pools data from 75 brands to help with translation, product briefs and image assets. She also set up an in-store sales focus group that uses AI to tighten teamwork and workflows, while insisting retail still rests on human contact — AI frees staff from chore work so they can study customer needs, not replace the handshake.

Telecom business development manager Ho Sze-no said her firm pushes AI for internal knowledge search, progress tracking and data analysis. Market-intelligence reports that once took about three weeks can now be drafted in about a week with AI helping gather sources, crunch numbers and sketch first drafts. She once feared the tools would hollow out her value; deeper use convinced her they are partners, freeing people for interaction, strategy and professional judgment that machines still cannot own.
