10th August 2026 - (Hong Kong) On Sunday, when the Observatory logged a record 36.9C, the Labour Department kept a yellow work heat warning all day and never stepped up to red or black — a pattern unchanged since the system launched in May 2023 — drawing fire from former Observatory director Lam Chiu-ying for under-weighting sun exposure on outdoor workers and for relying on the Observatory heat stress index instead of internationally used wet-bulb globe temperature.
Oxfam, which in 2024 designed a heat-alert system with Cyberport tech firm OrfiCon using roadside WBGT sensors, a network platform and smart watches that also pull Observatory alerts plus heart-rate and skin-temperature readings, argues WBGT better captures solar and ambient radiant heat, air temperature and humidity — the standard Singapore and Japan use, often with site instruments on large works. The Observatory index puts black-globe radiation at only about 5 per cent and natural wet-bulb factors near 80 per cent, and Labour warnings lean on King's Park; Oxfam plants meters beside cleaners and construction sites so readings match where people actually stand.
Between 23rd August and 10th September last year — 19 days — Oxfam with the Association for the Rights of Industrial Accident Victims and the Hong Kong Catholic Commission for Labour Affairs ran monitors in Yau Ma Tei, Kwun Tong, Aberdeen, Kennedy Town, Tsuen Wan, Kwai Chung and Tai Wo Hau Road, converting local WBGT into the department's colour bands for a January report. Labour issued yellow on only four of those days; the seven stations averaged 14 yellow days — about 3.5 times as many — and mean alert hours of 95.23, more than triple Labour's tally, with Tsuen Wan alone at 140 hours, over 110 hours above the official clock. Every station hit red and even black while Labour never left yellow: Kwun Tong logged six black and 11 red days; Kennedy Town three black and 13 red; even nearby pairs such as King's Park versus Yau Ma Tei, or Kwai Chung versus Tai Wo Hau Road, diverged, showing a single reference station can miss local peaks.
Work heat warnings are guidance, not direct criminal commands, though failing to manage heat that leads to an accident can breach the Occupational Safety and Health Ordinance. Of 64 outdoor cleaners surveyed, 94 per cent had at least one early heat-illness symptom in extreme heat; about 75 per cent said firms did not retimetable work, more than 35 per cent lacked enough drinking water, nearly 40 per cent had no special arrangements tied to alerts, nearly 70 per cent got a waist fan once with poor battery life and no waterproofing or follow-up, and nearly 97 per cent said employers never ran a separate on-site heat-stress assessment — a wide gap between paper guidance and the roadside.
