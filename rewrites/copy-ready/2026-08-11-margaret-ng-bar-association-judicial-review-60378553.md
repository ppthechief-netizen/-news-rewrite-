11th August 2026 - (Hong Kong) The Hong Kong Bar Association has asked the High Court to overturn a Barristers Disciplinary Tribunal ruling that cleared Margaret Ng Ngoi-yee of professional misconduct linked to her conviction for joining an unauthorised assembly in 2019, and wants the tribunal to admonish her even though she has retired.

Ng, a barrister, took part in the 18th August 2019 "flowing" assembly, was convicted of participating in an unauthorised assembly and received a suspended sentence. Someone later complained to the Bar Association of professional misconduct. The association referred the matter to a disciplinary tribunal, which this year found the complaint not established. Last Friday, 7th August, the association applied for judicial review, arguing the tribunal wrongly weighed Ng's political motives and that, in the public interest, the legal error should be corrected.

The applicant is the Bar Association, the respondent is the Barristers Disciplinary Tribunal, and Ng is an interested party. The tribunal was chaired by Senior Counsel Charlotte E. Draycott, sitting with barrister Jolie T.K. Chao and Sze Lai-shan.

According to the papers, Ng and others were convicted after trial of organising and participating in the unauthorised assembly of 18th August 2019 and given suspended sentences. The Court of Appeal later quashed the organising conviction while upholding the participation count; a further appeal to the Court of Final Appeal on that remaining conviction failed. The disciplinary tribunal on 8th June this year dismissed the misconduct complaint by a majority. The Bar Association says that decision was wrong on several grounds.

It argues the tribunal asked the wrong question — whether Ng was professionally at fault in a general sense, rather than whether she breached the Code of Conduct, which bars behaviour that may bring the barrister's profession into disrepute or weaken public confidence in it. Public confidence in the rule of law, the association says, rests on barristers' conduct; whatever their politics, they must respect and obey the law. In the criminal case the judge found Ng and others deliberately broke the law in a challenge to police and the legal order, and Ng was towards the front of the gathering. The association also says she advanced a false evacuation pretext as a defence at trial.

On that footing, it contends her criminal liability may bring the profession into disrepute or diminish public confidence, so the tribunal should have found a serious Code breach and serious misconduct. The association stresses the panel was not asked to pass moral judgment on political belief, only to decide legal issues, yet by considering Ng's political motives it entered a political arena and weighed irrelevant factors. Reasoning that she subjectively thought she was right, the association says, cannot save a deliberate breach of the law by a barrister.

Although Ng retired at the end of last year and no longer practises, the association says the public interest still requires the tribunal's legal error to be corrected so confidence in the profession is maintained. It asks the court to quash the present ruling, declare the complaint established, and have the tribunal admonish Ng.
