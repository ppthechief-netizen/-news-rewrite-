10th August 2026 - (Hong Kong) Under the peripheral sinking airflow of tropical cyclone White Dolphin, Hong Kong stayed locked in extreme heat this morning, and the Hong Kong Observatory issued a Very Hot Weather Warning at 6.45am before the Labour Department followed at 7.00am with another yellow work heat stress warning, again urging employers and employees to take heat-stroke precautions while the alert remains in force.

The department's work heat warnings track the Observatory's heat stress index: yellow when the index sits between 30 and below 32, red between 32 and 34, and black above 34. At 7.00am today the Labour Department repeated the yellow alert and told bosses and staff to take proper steps to stop heat stroke in very hot weather or high-temperature workplaces. Staff outdoors or indoors without air-conditioning face high heat stress and a relatively higher risk of heat stroke, so employers should assess risk factors at work and adopt prevention and control measures matching those risks, including rearranging work periods, putting up shade covers, providing ventilation and cooling equipment, and reminding staff to top up fluids and rest in time.

A Labour Department spokesman said that when a work heat warning is issued, employers must, while it is in force, follow the criteria and advice in the Guide on Preventing Heat Stroke at Work. For employees outdoors or in indoor workplaces without air-conditioning, bosses should assess physical workload and other heat-stress factors and, so far as reasonably practicable, arrange suitable rest each hour according to the warning level, cutting the chance of heat stroke. Employees must rest when told; anyone who develops heat-illness symptoms such as headache, dizziness, thirst or nausea should move at once to a cool shaded place, drink water and tell the employer or management so proper action can follow.

Former Observatory director Lam Chiu-ying posted on Facebook on Sunday criticising the system: with heat at a record-breaking pitch, the worker alert was still only yellow, and because heat stroke is not treated as an occupational injury, he said cancelling the scheme would hardly change anything. He argued it is wholly inappropriate for the Labour Department to use the Observatory heat stress index as the warning standard. That index, he explained, was drawn up years ago mainly to remind people who spend most of their time indoors to take anti-heat measures, not outdoor workers; the share given to solar radiation's black-globe temperature is extremely low and fails to reflect prolonged sun exposure for people working outside — in short, a yardstick for ordinary residents, not outdoor crews, so the department is using the wrong reference.

Many countries, he said, put far more weight on black-globe and wet-bulb temperatures when issuing workplace heat alerts — an international standard he cannot see why Hong Kong's Labour Department will not follow. He pointed to Japan: when workplace wet-bulb globe temperature (WBGT) reaches 28C or above, or air temperature exceeds 31C, and a worker stays in that environment continuously for more than one hour or for more than four hours in a day, the case strongly meets recognised workplace heat-stroke risk conditions, and heat stroke there counts as a work injury. He also called the thresholds for red and black warnings unrealistically high. Even with Sheung Shui hitting 39.8C this afternoon, he said, the calculated heat stress index was only 31 — still one degree short of red. If a record-breaking day like this still cannot produce red, he doubted red would appear for years to come.
