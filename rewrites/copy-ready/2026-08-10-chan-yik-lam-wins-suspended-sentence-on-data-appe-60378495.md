10th August 2026 - (Hong Kong) A male netizen who, after reading heated online debate about a concluded indecent-assault case, posted that he hoped someone in the know would "accidentally" let out the woman's particulars was charged with inciting others to disclose personal data, pleaded guilty and was sentenced to two months in prison.

He argued that the term was excessive and appealed, pointing to two earlier cases involving disclosure of protected persons' particulars in which defendants who had even published those details still received only suspended sentences. Deputy High Court Judge Cheung Kit-yee handed down reasons today, 10th August, holding that his comment drew only six likes and no replies; weighing that and his background, she allowed the appeal and substituted two months' imprisonment suspended for two years.

The appellant is Chan Yik-lam, 30, who worked for Watsons at the time of the offence. He admitted one count of inciting another person to disclose personal data without the data subject's consent and was jailed for two months at first instance. The court in the underlying case had ordered that the female complainant's identity must not be published; she is referred to as Woman X under that anonymity order.

In her reasons Judge Cheung said the prosecution grew from a finished criminal case in which Woman X was the complainant protected by the anonymity order. After that verdict the matter drew wide online discussion, including demands to reveal X's identity and personal data.

On 7th May 2024 a netizen posted dissatisfaction with the ruling and collected 15,000 likes. The same day Mr Chan used his account to write that he hoped an informed person would "accidentally" speak out the girl's details — wording that matched the remark later put to him in the charge — and the comment attracted six likes.

When X learned of the defendant's post she feared her personal data might be exposed. She dared not return to her own home, stayed at a friend's for four days without going out, suffered insomnia for two weeks because of the episode, and feared going outdoors or that she and her family would be harassed.

Mr Chan was arrested in March 2025. Under caution he said he did not know X's identity, that he left the comment on impulse after seeing fierce online debate, and that he had not realised the remark might incite others to disclose her personal data.

On appeal he advanced several grounds. He said the trial court wrongly treated the case as lacking special circumstances and refused to consider a suspended sentence or a community service order. He said the court rejected, without evidence, his claim that the post was a momentary slip. He also cited two cases involving online disclosure of personal data or breaches of anonymity orders in which the defendants had gone further and actually published protected persons' particulars, yet still received suspended sentences — while he had a clean record at the time of the offence and therefore, he argued, faced an excessive term.

Judge Cheung held that offences of this kind challenge and interfere with the court's administration of justice, harm the public interest, the rule of law and public confidence in the judiciary, and must attract deterrent sentences so that similar conduct is discouraged and future victims are not frightened into silence about reporting crime or giving evidence.

Incitement on social media, she added, is an aggravating feature, so immediate imprisonment was in principle the right starting point. Yet, given Mr Chan's good character and clean record, and that the comment attracted only six likes with nobody responding, she found suspension available in this case and substituted two months' imprisonment suspended for two years.

According to available records, two other netizens who left similar comments pleaded guilty and were each sentenced only to 120 hours of community service.
