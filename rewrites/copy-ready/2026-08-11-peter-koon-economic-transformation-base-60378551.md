11th August 2026 - (Hong Kong) Election Committee legislator the Honourable Peter Douglas Koon has warned, in a newspaper commentary as the five-year planning public consultation closes on Friday 14th August, that an economic transformation focused only on upstream high-tech industries will leave deeper social contradictions untreated if public resources do not tilt fairly toward grass-roots livelihoods.

Koon argued that restructuring cannot be the "exclusive joy" of a few upstream sectors, technology giants or elite talent. Middle-aged and older workers who dominate much of the labour market, together with micro, small and medium enterprises, cannot retrain or retool overnight, he wrote, asking how such groups are supposed to transform with ease.

If most wage-earners feel sidelined in the transition and cannot share the dividends of growth, he cautioned, more complex social friction will follow. Macroeconomic indicators, he added, often look healthier on statistical dashboards than they feel in the daily lives of grass-roots households and traditional trades.

Hong Kong is in a critical phase of structural change, yet how long the shift will take and how painful the middle years will be remain unknown. Pouring resources into upstream industries, innovation and high-value services is a necessary way to defend competitiveness, Koon acknowledged, but the ultimate aim of economic development should be to ease deep-seated social problems.

Housing strain and rising welfare and medical burdens, he wrote, rarely stem from an absolute shortage of resources so much as from skewed allocation. The harder question is whether the transformation lets the wider public enjoy the fruits of development — a test that mid-career workers, small firms and traditional service staff will struggle to pass if technical and skills shifts are demanded at breakneck speed.

A responsible government, Koon said, must strike a precise balance between chasing growth and safeguarding social fairness. Authorities should actively consult trade unions, small-business chambers, the social-welfare sector and other stakeholders, and keep the reform path inclusive so that most residents benefit. Only then, he concluded, can Hong Kong walk the transformation road more steadily.

His intervention lands as officials gather views for the next planning cycle, pressing them to measure success not only by headline GDP or flagship tech projects but by whether ordinary workers and smaller firms still see a place in the new economy. Without that shared stake, Koon's warning runs, statistical progress can coexist with unresolved grievance.
