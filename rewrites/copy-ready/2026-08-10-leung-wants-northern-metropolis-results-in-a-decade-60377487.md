10th August 2026 - (Hong Kong) With the national 15th Five-Year Plan pressing Hong Kong to accelerate the Northern Metropolis, and the SAR government this year proposing dedicated Northern Metropolis legislation to cut red tape, Bills Committee chairwoman Prof Hon Priscilla Leung Mei-fun told an exclusive interview that Shanghai remade Pudong within about ten years and that Hong Kong must likewise see clear Northern Metropolis results inside a decade.

She also urged that when Hong Kong writes its own five-year plan it should set overall and staged Northern Metropolis targets, and that if a mid-term review shows those targets are missed the government should loosen the rules further.

The government earlier tabled the Northern Metropolis Development Bill, proposing dedicated primary legislation plus subsidiary rules under which the Chief Executive in Council could, as needed, make regulations in six areas: simplifying town-planning procedures; speeding land-resumption compensation payments; innovative building techniques; flexible noise control; easier cross-boundary movement; and setting up park companies. The bill received its first reading last month and the Bills Committee is now scrutinising it.

On why a dedicated statute matters, Ms Leung said the Northern Metropolis offers huge opportunity and must move faster and more efficiently, and that in a rule-of-law society reform has to put law first — as mainland special economic zones also began by clearing legal obstacles. Dedicated legislation, she argued, would give departments a legal basis to shorten approvals where discretion allows and to tackle today's bottlenecks in infrastructure and people flows.

Asked how much faster delivery would become after the law takes effect, she said the gain is hard to measure simply because the statute's job is to set rules and support education and promotion. She recalled visiting Pudong years ago when it resembled today's Northern Metropolis, yet a decade later it had advanced sharply. Within ten years, she said, the Northern Metropolis must show achievements such as unicorn companies or international organisations locating there.

She voiced strong confidence because the zone sits between Hong Kong's international financial centre and Shenzhen's innovation-and-technology hub — much as Pudong once leaned on powerful Puxi. A new district cannot flourish if it is handed over casually, she said; it must cling to a place with real potential and strength. That is why staged targets in the five-year plan, with further deregulation if mid-term checks fail, matter.

Once the dedicated law applies, some fields will follow rules different from the rest of Hong Kong. Ms Leung rejected the idea that the Northern Metropolis would become a "special zone within a special zone", but said a new development area needs more room to grow. Many mature districts already struggle to loosen noise or other controls because nearby residents object, whereas the Northern Metropolis statute would ease building-noise regulation by extending construction noise permits from six months to one year and allowing flexible deployment of plant within set noise standards to lift construction efficiency.

Whether the dedicated regime could later expand territory-wide is premature, she said, because the changes affect people who live and trade across Hong Kong; any wider roll-out should wait until the pilot succeeds — mirroring how the mainland often trials new laws before broader reform.

Some worry that empowering government to make subsidiary legislation first and seek LegCo scrutiny later would leave the law under-supervised. Ms Leung said weak public confidence in a new project is normal, but insisted that even under that procedure the government still has time to hear members and that the making of subsidiary rules remains under public watch. She cited the pandemic episode in which ministers were empowered to declare vaccination medical exemptions invalid under a similar "make first, scrutinise later" path, later facing judicial review — evidence, she said, of a mature legal system in which nobody simply "crosses the sea and becomes an immortal". LegCo must scrutinise subsidiary legislation carefully and must not treat it lightly, she added, because nobody wants another "seat-belt incident".
