11th August 2026 - (Hong Kong) Outdoor construction workers say Hong Kong's yellow Work Heat Warning feels far too mild when temperatures push towards 40 degrees Celsius, and that whether they can rest still depends on their employer even as the Labour Department keeps the alert in force.

With strong typhoon Bailutang's outer subsiding air keeping the city extremely hot on 10th August, reporters found crews on several sites "fully armoured" against the sun — sun hats, ice sleeves, sweat towels, fan shirts and several litres of warm water or sports drinks a day. One worker put it bluntly: keeping the warning at yellow when the mercury is almost at 40 degrees Celsius is "going too far".

Miss Lam, a site worker, wore a sun hat, sun mask and ice sleeves with a towel stuffed at her back to mop sweat that dried and soaked again. She said she drinks at least three litres a day and sticks to warm water, fearing that cold drinks in hot conditions make heatstroke more likely. Seasoned colleagues with tougher skin cope better, she added, but many younger newcomers struggle to adapt.

Two other workers said they get through four to six large bottles of sports drink or oolong tea each day. Mud worker Mr Chan wore a purpose-made fan shirt and said switching the fans on greatly eased how the heat felt on his body.

Several workers argued the official yellow guide does not match what they feel on the ground. Miss Lam, who lives in Sheung Shui, said the district hit 39.8 degrees Celsius the day before; even on her day off, shopping outdoors, the radiant heat from the pavement was intense and she dared not stay out long. In such extremes, she said, a red or black work heat warning is needed. Mr Shing, four years in scaffolding, said last week's heat already warranted red or even black alerts and urged a more humane threshold, repeating that a yellow warning near 40 degrees Celsius is excessive.

Labour Department advice tells employers to arrange rest in hot weather, yet frontline staff say practice varies by company. Nepalese worker Gurung, more than twenty years in the trade, said breaks follow the boss: some push hard and ignore conditions, others tell crews to slow down, rest and drink. One larger contractor, workers said, offers an air-conditioned container office open to all grades, a drinks machine with rehydration beverages, and recent rotations of half an hour on and half an hour off — or fifteen minutes' work and forty-five minutes' rest at the hottest times — though crews still cut rest when jobs are rushed.
