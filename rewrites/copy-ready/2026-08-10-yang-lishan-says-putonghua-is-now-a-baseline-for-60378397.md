10th August 2026 - (Hong Kong) In an HK01 forum essay, CPPCC member Yang Lishan argues that behind Xia Baolong's smile when he urged Hong Kong legislators to "cram" Putonghua after their Beijing study trip sits a real communications gap — and that for those who now administer Hong Kong, national common speech has moved from a bonus skill to a baseline duty of office.

Hong Kong has long advertised "two written languages, three spoken", she writes, yet Cantonese still dominates more than 90 per cent of daily settings, while most adults "understand some Mandarin yet expose themselves the moment they open their mouths." Generations over 35 grew up under colonial schooling with little systematic Putonghua; after the handover primary and secondary schools added the subject, but many classrooms still "teach Putonghua through Cantonese", so results stay thin. The result is a familiar spectacle: officials and lawmakers speaking Mandarin, stalling on key terms, muttering first in Cantonese, patching with English, forcing out Putonghua, then joking that "my Putonghua is very ordinary."

On this Beijing trip many members had five-minute speaking slots flagged in advance and scripts in hand, yet listeners still heard Cantonese "read a little crooked". Colleagues joked that some "Hong Kong-style Putonghua", while short of a Louis Koo caricature, was unintelligible to non-locals — and that the whole hall might have been decoded seamlessly only by Guangxi-born Hong Kong and Macao Affairs Office deputy director Nong Rong, who understands Cantonese. The pattern, Ms Yang says, is not a one-off; Mr Xia's laugh-and-nudge named nobody and cooled the room with precise manners, yet the joke landed because it pricked a fact: representing Hong Kong in Beijing on the 15th Five-Year Plan, the Northern Metropolis or innovation docking while central officials must guess your policy case is an asymmetric exchange.

She insists criticism must stay fair. Lawmakers are not diplomats; Cantonese is their electoral base language and rightful for district work, and Cantonese speech inside the Legislative Council is protected by the rules and need not be abolished. The gap is not "speaking Cantonese" but whether, when engagement with the national system is required, members have minimum common-language capacity. After "patriots administering Hong Kong", the 90-seat council — 88 members on this trip — was screened as a constructive legislature expected to work with the SAR government, central ministries and mainland provinces and cities on the Northern Metropolis, Greater Bay Area rule alignment, innovation chains and youth employment. LegCo President Starry Lee Wai-king, summing up the study programme, said members must "stand in the bigger picture and keep the country in mind"; Mr Xia both affirmed the council's work and pressed stronger study to support the government in easing people's "urgent difficulties and worries". When studying national development plans becomes part of the job, Putonghua is the transfer layer from text to speech and from a Hong Kong chamber to the Great Hall of the People.

Privately, she notes, some members said that after "crossing five passes and slaying six generals" to win seats, Putonghua "should not be at this level" — a sharper line than media teasing. Election vetting proves political loyalty and eligibility, not automatic cross-context communication; the latter is learned. With notice, scripts and preparation time, drilling characters and tones before travel was the least owed; carrying unexamined "Hong Kong-style Putonghua" into the Great Hall without self-correction is what invites ridicule.

Language, she argues, is an interface of "integration". Some read Mr Xia's words as a coded rebuke of last year's Cantonese-speaking episode involving Tommy Cheung Yu-yan and others, but political circles also admit that, more than a year on, treating one smile as delayed score-settling over-reads the moment. The plainer reading is that Beijing treats LegCo as a key element of Hong Kong's high degree of autonomy under "one country, two systems"; Mr Xia sitting through five hours to hear nearly half the delegation speak was itself rare courtesy — and under that courtesy he marked the homework of common-language capacity: patriotism is not only a stance; governing Hong Kong also needs tools.

Those tools matter in three directions. Toward the mainland: if Hong Kong is to dock with the 15th Five-Year Plan and catch the window of new quality productive forces, lawmakers who cannot put clear Putonghua questions in talks, field visits or signing ceremonies lose a layer of information. Former deputies once joked in the National People's Congress Hong Kong group that their Putonghua was "too ordinary", yet nobody spoke Cantonese in that chamber and materials with pinyin still had to be voiced — a baseline self-awareness for SAR representation; with 88 members on a higher-spec trip, that self-awareness should be stronger. Toward Hong Kong society: speaking Putonghua does not abandon Cantonese; it shows "two written, three spoken" is not a shop sign. When young people see a lawmaker discuss housing in Putonghua in the Great Hall, they grasp more intuitively than in class that Mandarin is an ascent path; Singaporean Chinese commonly hold bilingualism, and Hong Kong has no reason to lock Putonghua forever as only a "mainlanders' language". Toward council culture: the new legislature has left "mutual destruction" for homework, bills and departmental replies; Tan Yueheng asking in Putonghua, Chief Executive John Lee Ka-chiu answering in Putonghua, and Sun Dong swearing and speaking in Putonghua are normalising fragments — Mr Xia's smile, she says, is a shove toward that normalisation, so members need not wait for the next Beijing visit to be laughed at and can open more Putonghua slots in ordinary council life.

Cramming Putonghua is not shameful; it is doing the job. Mr Xia did not farm listening to translators, did not cut speakers off, and sat five hours through a sea of Hong Kong-style Mandarin — the mildest inclusion. Inclusion does not erase standards: central officials understanding you is not the same as your case landing cleanly; colleagues' ears are not mainland provinces, ministries or firms. Practical fixes she lists are clear: Putonghua script proofing in members' offices and recorded rehearsals before key speeches; LegCo Putonghua drills, especially for functional-constituency members who often visit the mainland and need chairing and questioning basics; normalising national-affairs study with pre-trip pronunciation and terminology boot camps — "new quality productive forces", "common prosperity" and "high-level opening-up" cannot be bluffed with Cantonese homophones; and a social tone that stops mocking imperfect speakers without romanticising unintelligible "Hong Kong accents" — accents can stay, empty policy wording cannot.

Stringing this Beijing laugh together with the wider shift, she concludes that Putonghua for Hong Kong's governing elite is already a baseline, not a bonus. Mr Xia used a joke to save face; if the next trip shows no progress, the joke becomes a harder question about whether five years of learning the language of national governance ever began. After the laughter, language should count as part of the job: Cantonese is Hong Kong's soul and need not be abolished; English is the international shell and must not be discarded; Putonghua is the national common language and must be usable — coexistence is not lying flat. The 88 members studied the 15th Five-Year Plan and Xi Jinping Thought and toured Zhongguancun and party-history exhibitions, gains of horizon; Mr Xia's smiling reminder is a gain of method — horizon without speech is only half a term of office. Do not only share headlines about him "unable to suppress a laugh". Ask instead how many dare put a Northern Metropolis question in Putonghua at the next interactive session — and whether, opposite Mr Xia next time, colleagues still need Mr Nong as decoder. Language is never small; it is the thermometer of whether a political group will truly enter another governance lexicon. If Hong Kong is serious about integrating into national development, she closes, start with lawmakers reading their scripts accurately.
