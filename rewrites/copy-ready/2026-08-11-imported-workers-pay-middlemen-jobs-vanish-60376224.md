11th August 2026 - (Hong Kong) Nearly 110,000 imported workers have been approved under the Enhanced Supplementary Labour Scheme in almost two years, yet an investigation finds third-party middlemen — known colloquially as "boat boys" — layering fees, void contracts and illegal work onto what should be a tightly regulated path.

Mainland labour for the scheme must go through one of 14 state-approved labour firms, or their licensed Hong Kong employment agencies, and workers pay those companies a labour service fee. When a middleman also brokers the deal, another intermediary fee is added. Workers who applied via a Zhuhai agency called Xin Li Liang say they paid tens of thousands of yuan and still ended up with invalid paperwork, employers who denied hiring them, or jobs that breached visa conditions. Some who never started, or have already returned to the mainland, say the labour firm refunded the agent — yet nearly RMB200,000 chased from the middleman has not come back.

Mr He, Mr Wang, Ah Ming (not his real name) and five other mainlanders were hired in 2025 through Xin Li Liang as "drivers" and "dispatchers" for a Hong Kong logistics firm. Each paid RMB36,000 to RMB38,000 in combined labour and agency fees. Online interviews and contract signing never put them face to face with the employer.

After signing in September they heard nothing. In December the agent took them for a walk around Kwai Chung Container Terminal; men claiming to be company staff described work areas and licence types the firm would sponsor. In March 2026 Immigration issued work visas naming the Hong Kong employer, with contracts bearing a signature and company chop, but no start date came. When they rang the firm, staff said no imported workers had been hired and denied signing. "I wonder how this visa was approved," Mr He said.

Of the eight who got visas, only two have partial refunds; the three interviewed have recovered nothing. The logistics firm had not replied by publication. Immigration and the Labour Department gave no case details.

Ms Wong, placed via Xin Li Liang at a drinks chain, had a contract listing Yuen Long but was sent to Taikoo, Kowloon Bay and Kwun Tong shops — not the handcrafted lemon-tea brand named in the contract but another yoghurt brand run by the same employer. Within two months a fellow imported worker from the same agent was arrested at work. Immigration confirmed a 2025 arrest at the Kowloon Bay Telford Plaza branch of a Chinese worker suspected of working off the contracted site; only the worker was charged with breach of condition of stay and later bound over. The employer was not prosecuted.

Under the scheme, workers must stay at contracted addresses. Employers may apply for up to three designated sites, raised to five from June 2026; without approval, off-site work is treated as illegal. Ms Wong said the agent messaged workers to report to various shops and later claimed not to know the arrangement would turn legal labour illegal — a denial she rejects. After the arrest she insisted on the contract address, was sacked within a fortnight without reason, and still awaits a refund she says the labour firm already returned to the middleman. "I paid more than RMB30,000 to work legally," she said. "Why pay that to become an illegal worker?"

Two other women hired as kitchen helper and cleaner said they were put on floor waiting duties instead — also unlawful. One recovered the labour fee from the labour firm but not the agency cut. Workers who cannot reclaim money say Xin Li Liang brokered them through Fujian Zhongfu Foreign Labour Cooperation Co., Ltd.; Zhongfu's Hong Kong subsidiary said it operates lawfully and would not shirk proven responsibility, but did not explain its link to Xin Li Liang.

Mr Fung, a Guangzhou salesman with more than a decade in retail, paid only half — RMB17,000 — before coming. After nearly half a year with no start date he found his Enhanced Supplementary Labour Scheme standard contract incomplete: he had signed, but only one of three employer signature boxes was filled and Labour Department fields were blank without a stamp. The department confirmed stamped forms are issued only after quotas are formally approved, so the paper he paid on was unfinished and invalid. The named employer said he had asked Xin Li Liang to seek quotas after video interviews, kept pressing for arrivals through early 2026, then walked away when the delay and the agent looked suspicious and never finished the visa paperwork. Another restaurant case likewise involved an unsigned, unstamped contract and an unpaid RMB30,000 agency fee; the restaurant said it never signed that worker and pointed reporters back to the middleman.
