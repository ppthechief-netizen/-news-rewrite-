10th August 2026 - (Hong Kong) Artificial intelligence is shifting from a simple helper to a workplace partner, and a Hong Kong University of Science and Technology survey of private-sector staff finds that 73 per cent of respondents use AI daily or weekly — well above a cited global average of 31 per cent — while about six in ten have never had AI training and nearly eight in ten want firms or the government to set clearer frameworks and regulatory guidance.

HKUST released the findings today from an online poll run between March and April this year that gathered 3,722 valid responses from people working in commercial organisations, excluding government and non-governmental bodies. More than 70 per cent of respondents held a university degree or higher; most were aged 30 to 49, the sexes were roughly evenly split, and the sample spanned finance, information technology, construction and engineering, accounting and auditing, healthcare and other trades across ranks and firm sizes. Some 82 per cent said they use generative tools such as ChatGPT, Copilot and Gemini daily or weekly, mainly for writing, document work, data analysis and content summaries. About 72 per cent mostly rely on free public AI tools, while only about 35 per cent — roughly half that share — use licensed systems subscribed and deployed by their employers, and just 20 per cent use in-house or third-party-built proprietary AI. Adoption is uneven: large firms, middle and senior staff, managers and team leaders use AI more deeply, while about 26 per cent of respondents in small firms said they do not use it at all. Research and development, information technology, insurance, and energy and utilities were more optimistic about AI; retail, hotels and tourism, healthcare, logistics and telecoms lagged. Asked about benefits, 64.4 per cent pointed to higher efficiency and productivity, 45.9 per cent to help with repetitive chores, and 42.1 per cent to better quality and accuracy. Nearly 44 per cent said AI had lifted productivity without changing their core duties, while more than one in ten — 12 per cent — already fold reviewing, supervising or correcting AI output into daily work as human-machine collaboration becomes part of the workflow.

Staff also voiced worries. The top concern was inaccurate AI-generated content, followed by privacy and information security, then the spread of false information. About half — 49 per cent — fear their skills will be devalued by AI, and about 40 per cent worry about job security. Middle and senior staff more often cast AI as a chance to lift capability, remake processes and create opportunity, while front-line workers more often fear skill erosion or replacement. Thirty per cent expect AI to reshape their work heavily within three to five years, and another 30 per cent say that shift has already arrived or will within one to two years. Knowledge-intensive fields — research and development, information technology, media and communications, law, marketing and advertising, and insurance — more often said change is already here or due within one to two years. Law, media and communications, and marketing and advertising respondents were most anxious about skills devaluation, at between 60 and 70 per cent, while media and communications, financial services, and scientific and technical services showed relatively higher job-security worry.

Professor Kar Yan Tam, HKUST's vice-president for administration and business, said there is not yet data showing AI has sharply cut entry-level posts for new graduates, and that job shifts may also reflect the broader economy. Full integration of AI into workflows remains early, he added, and often still rests at the level of individual tool use. A job usually bundles many tasks: AI may automate the more repetitive, time-consuming slices without replacing the whole role, and firms that adopt it must redefine duties — some old tasks shrink while new ones appear, such as verifying AI output, supervising systems and fixing errors. Fact-checking AI content and final decisions must stay with people, he argued. In customer service, for example, AI can quickly assemble a client's past interactions so front-line staff grasp background and preferences, but talking to the client, handling emotion and making judgments remain human work. Skills upgrading, he said, must be shared: firms should not stop at generic online courses but design role-specific training — teaching customer-service staff to analyse interaction logs with AI, or helping analysts verify and improve AI output — while employees should tell employers what they want to learn and which new tasks they can take on after roles are redesigned.

The survey found about 60 per cent of respondents have never received AI training yet want to learn, citing lack of time (50.3 per cent), not knowing where to start (47.9 per cent) and struggling to find paths suited to their posts (46.6 per cent). On institutional support, 73 per cent said they were unaware of government AI policies, laws or guidance, and only 28 per cent said their organisations already had related measures — leaving many without internal AI rules — while 79 per cent want companies or the government to supply clear AI frameworks and regulatory guidance. HKUST urged firms to build or refresh AI-use frameworks covering privacy, information security, accuracy checks, handling of false information and human-machine workflows, with clear lines of responsibility for reviewing, supervising and correcting AI output, and to give staff learning time, starter guides and training resources so they need not invent their own path.
