10th August 2026 - (Hong Kong) After the Wang Fuk Court fire, residents fixated on a HK$2 billion owners' corporation "fire" policy — formally building structural cover — and whether payouts die if the government refuses on-site rebuilding or buys claim rights with the titles; HK01 earlier obtained the contract, which resembles ordinary fire wording, including a statutory-compliance warranty that insured parties must meet Fire Services and Labour Department rules or risk breach, and a reinstatement clause that in principle allows works at another location.

Hong Kong Federation of Insurers chief executive Selina Lau Pui-ling, without commenting on that specific disclosed contract, walked through how such policies work, why the inquiry must come first, and how buyouts, public liability and home contents sit beside shell cover. Structural fire insurance, she explained, protects the flat "shell" as handed over — walls, ceilings, floors, doors and windows — the standard finish at handover rather than later decoration.

The Wang Fuk policy, bought by the corporation through the management office, blankets some 2,000 flats and common parts including corridors, rear stairs, lift shafts and pipework, and can respond to typhoon facade damage as well as fire — it is not a fire-only product. Dividing HK$2 billion by household into HK$1 million each is a myth, Ms Lau said: the product restores units toward their pre-loss, roughly 50-year-old Home Ownership Scheme condition rather than cashing out paper losses.

The blaze is unprecedented and extremely complex; if flats will not be reinstated as before — the government has ruled out rebuilding on site and is acquiring titles with claim rights attached — insurers must renegotiate valuation with the owner by estimating what reinstatement would have cost, which is one reason meaningful structural claims wait on the independent committee. Policy wording can allow reinstatement at another location in principle, but the practical post-buyout path remains under discussion between government and insurers.

Investigators have already aired flammable foam, non-flame-retardant scaffolding nets and allegedly disabled fire mains and alarms during major works. Ms Lau said payouts need the inquiry — and possibly later criminal probes — to pin down cause and whether works breached warranties such as non-compliant or non-fireproof materials, because human or non-compliant factors can shrink or reshape indemnity. With so many alleged breaches, she asked whether human factors must be weighed; only after the report and legal process will insurers decide how, and how much, to pay amid heavy uncertainty over method and quantum.

She understands owners who feel claim rights are being "gifted" to the government, yet argues a buyer of unrepaired fire-damaged flats reasonably takes the insurance with them — and that buyout prices already partly bank expected recoveries, pre-paying disaster victims so they need not wait years for cash. She expects the legal and claims path to be extremely tangled, citing London's 2017 Grenfell Tower fire, where insurance recovery only recently closed even under single ownership and would be harder with fragmented titles. Handing claim rights with the titles, she said, can spare small owners a long slog while government and insurers negotiate and households restart their lives.

Unburnt Wang Chi House still complicates how much of the estate pot government and insurers must apportion after sales — that share must be negotiated once the government holds the titles. Separate personal fire policies share reinstatement under a once-only principle rather than paying twice — the two insurers would bargain over the unit's reinstatement value — yet how those sit beside the corporation rights the government is taking remains unsettled.

Statutory public liability — at least HK$10 million, topped to HK$20 million — and about HK$50 million of contractors' all-risks may help third parties such as cars crushed by scaffolding once main and sub-contractor fault is sorted, another long legal road after the inquiry assigns responsibility. Home contents insurers have already paid above HK$500 million by early February under special no-receipt arrangements for owners and tenants' fittings, furniture, appliances and personal effects, clearing nearly nine-tenths of affected cases at limits often HK$800,000 to HK$1.2 million, plus HK$80,000 to HK$100,000 alternative-accommodation stipends and even spoiled freezer food.

Looking ahead, Ms Lau expects tighter independent checks on fire alarms when estates buy public-liability or building fire cover — premiums may rise where overnight total loss of more than a thousand flats looks plausible, or fall if management proves vigilant instead of casual. She urges households to buy suitable contents cover for extreme weather and fire, and to tell trusted relatives, spouses or solicitors their policy numbers and insurers before the next crisis so someone can chase claims or emergency help when the insured cannot.
