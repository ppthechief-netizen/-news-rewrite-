10th August 2026 - (Hong Kong) CPPCC member Yang Lishan writes that Xia Baolong's smiling push for legislators to drill Putonghua after their Beijing study trip exposes a real communications gap: Hong Kong's "two written, three spoken" slogan still leaves Cantonese dominating daily life while many adults understand Mandarin yet stumble aloud — especially those schooled in colonial years, and even post-handover classrooms that sometimes teach Putonghua through Cantonese.

Lawmakers who reached the Great Hall with five-minute scripts still sounded like Cantonese "read a little crooked"; colleagues joked that only Guangxi-born deputy director Nong Rong, who understands Cantonese, could decode some "Hong Kong-style Putonghua" short of Louis Koo caricature. Mr Xia's joke named nobody and cooled the room, she argues, yet landed because speaking for Hong Kong on the 15th Five-Year Plan, the Northern Metropolis or innovation links while central officials must guess is asymmetric. Cantonese remains rightful for districts and LegCo rules; the gap is minimum common-language capacity when engaging the national system. After patriots administering Hong Kong, 88 of 90 seats went north as a constructive legislature meant to work with the SAR government, central ministries and mainland cities — Starry Lee Wai-king urged members to stand in the bigger picture, and Mr Xia both praised LegCo and pressed study to ease people's urgent hardships. Election vetting proves loyalty, not cross-context speech; with notice and scripts, drilling characters before travel was the least they owed.

She rejects reading last year's Tommy Cheung Cantonese episode as delayed score-settling, preferring the plainer view that five hours of listening was rare courtesy — and courtesy still sets homework: Putonghua as a tool of governance, not merely a stance. That tool matters toward the mainland so questions on new quality productive forces are not lost; toward Hong Kong society so youths see Mandarin as an ascent path beside Cantonese soul and English shell, as Singapore's bilingualism shows; and toward a council culture already normalising Mandarin Q&A from Tan Yueheng, Chief Executive John Lee Ka-chiu and Sun Dong. Remedies she floats include office script coaching and recorded rehearsals, LegCo Putonghua practice especially for functional constituencies that visit the mainland, pre-trip drills on national-policy terms, and a social tone that tolerates accents but not unintelligible policy. The real test, she closes, is how many dare ask a Northern Metropolis question in Putonghua at the next interactive session — and whether Mr Xia next time still needs Mr Nong as decoder.
