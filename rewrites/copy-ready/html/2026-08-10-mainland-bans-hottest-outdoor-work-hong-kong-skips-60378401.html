10th August 2026 - (Hong Kong) On Sunday, 9th August, when the Hong Kong Observatory logged a record 36.9C, the Labour Department held a yellow work heat warning all day, and former Observatory director Lam Chiu-ying again pressed the case that solar radiation is under-weighted and that Hong Kong should switch to the internationally common wet-bulb globe temperature (WBGT); since the work heat system launched in 2023, red and black warnings have never been issued.

A survey of heat rules elsewhere shows how far that gap runs. On the mainland, the Measures for the Administration of Heat Prevention and Cooling Measures are mandatory regulations: when air temperature is at or above 35C and below 37C, employers should rotate shifts and shorten continuous working time; when temperature is at or above 37C and below 40C, cumulative outdoor open-air work must not exceed six hours, and outdoor open-air work is banned during the hottest three-hour period; when temperature reaches 40C or above, outdoor open-air work for that day must stop. The same international toolkit centres on WBGT, which combines solar and nearby environmental radiant heat, air temperature and relative humidity into a composite heat-stress reading used as an outdoor stop-work indicator.

Taiwanese law allocates work and rest by the hour according to light, moderate or heavy workload and changes in the composite heat index (WBGT) that folds in temperature, humidity and wind speed. High-temperature workplaces may not exceed six hours of work a day, and work-to-rest ratios are set by labour intensity: for heavy work, when WBGT reaches 32.1C, rest must take up 75 per cent of the time, and employers who breach the rules face administrative penalties.

Singapore likewise treats WBGT as a statutory standard for stop-work and rest for outdoor staff doing heavy physical labour. Violating a stop-work order can bring a fine of up to S$500,000 and imprisonment for one year. Specified outdoor workplaces — including large construction sites, shipyards and processing sites — must install WBGT instruments. When WBGT reaches 32C or above, workers must take at least ten continuous minutes of rest each hour in a shaded place; at 33C or above the mandatory rest rises to at least fifteen continuous minutes each hour; and as the index climbs further, high-risk employees must be moved off outdoor posts.

Hong Kong's work heat warnings track the Observatory's heat stress index, calculated by a set formula originally designed to show when temperature, humidity and similar factors raise sudden hospital admissions among the general public. Because most residents spend daily life indoors, the formula gives black-globe temperature — solar radiation — only about 5 per cent weight, while natural wet-bulb factors for humidity and wind take about 80 per cent. Mr Lam says work heat warnings should instead use black-globe and wet-bulb temperatures suited to open-air settings and already standard worldwide, and he calls it "out of touch" that Labour still withholds a higher warning even when air temperature breaks records.

The department's Guide on Prevention of Heat Stroke at Work and the work heat warning system, both built on the Hong Kong heat stress index, launched on 15th May 2023 and have been "optimised" twice, including lengthening the heat-index monitoring window from 30 minutes to one hour and, beyond the King's Park station, folding in heat-index readings from nine other locations across the territory. The guide and the warnings are not compulsory: employers may cut or adjust staff rest time while a warning is in force if they judge that cooling measures already match the actual workplace. The guidance carries no direct legal force, yet if an employee is hurt the department can still prosecute under the general employer duties in the Occupational Safety and Health Ordinance.

Under the same guide, light work covers security guards, property managers and florists; medium work covers cleaners and couriers; heavy work covers movers and steel-fixers; and extremely heavy work means special high-exertion physical labour. While a red work heat warning is in force, extremely heavy workers must pause; when a black warning is in force, both heavy and extremely heavy workers must pause.

Oxfam's study published early this year quoted workers saying that colleagues who sat down to rest were complained about, so — following contractors' "reminders" — they held a water bottle while seated to prove they were resting rather than "slacking", a response they called putting the cart before the horse. Unions also said some contractors, worried about company logos being covered, barred workers from wearing vest fans recommended by the Occupational Safety and Health Council, and workers reported that employers still offered no special arrangements for extreme weather — leaving Hong Kong with advisory colour bands while mainland, Taiwan and Singapore already hard-wire WBGT thresholds into rest, shade and stop-work law.
