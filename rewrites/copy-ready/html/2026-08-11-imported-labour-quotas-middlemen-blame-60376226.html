11th August 2026 - (Hong Kong) Roughly 110,000 imported-worker quotas have been approved under the Enhanced Supplementary Labour Scheme since its launch, yet the same period has brought repeated cases of rogue middlemen exploiting labourers and even pushing some into illegal work.

Rules require state-designated labour agencies to handle formalities for workers coming to Hong Kong, but third-party brokers — known colloquially as "boat boys" — still insert themselves between employers and workers for profit. Whether those brokers operate in co-operation with designated labour firms remains contested. Legislative Council member Chau Siu-chung says a grey area may exist and that Hong Kong and mainland authorities should build a notification channel for unusual cases, while the Labour Department should follow up when suspicious patterns appear and require Hong Kong employers to explain.

In under three years the scheme has approved about 110,000 imported-labour places. By 31st May 2026, 74,000 people had been granted visas and 67,000 had already arrived. Throughout that expansion, workers have reported high agency fees, monthly salary kickbacks and withheld refunds demanded by third-party middlemen.

In 2024, Secretary for Labour and Welfare Mr Chris SUN Yuk-han, JP, told the Legislative Council that the bureau worked closely with China's Ministry of Commerce to keep labour companies compliant on both sides of the boundary, while warning that however tightly a system is drawn, "someone will always look for loopholes". In a Wen Wei Po interview he urged workers to apply directly through designated labour agencies so the process stayed within both jurisdictions' laws, yet some labourers say it is hard to secure posts that way.

Chau Siu-chung notes that direct applications to the 14 designated labour enterprises can mean fiercer competition, while middlemen can dangle other openings. Mainland rules bar firms outside those 14 from handling Hong Kong-related labour business. Earlier reporting found Zhuhai middleman Xin Li Liang gathering clients and then routing paperwork through a designated labour firm; when contracts collapsed, refunds went back to the middleman rather than straight to workers, leaving labourers chasing money that never arrived. Chau believes that arrangement may sit in a grey zone, but says the Hong Kong government cannot rewrite mainland law and can mainly strengthen publicity and education for workers.

He argues that if Hong Kong and mainland departments share alerts when unusual clusters appear, mainland agencies could move against illegal conduct more quickly. On Xin Li Liang cases in which workers were told to report to sites not named in their contracts — turning them into illegal workers — or found job scopes different from those agreed, Chau says departments should step up post-arrival education so labourers refuse demands that breach contracts and visa conditions.

Among mainland workers contacted for the series, at least eight obtained Immigration work visas naming Hong Kong companies that later denied hiring imported staff. Employers must pay a retraining levy of about HK$9,000 for each imported worker before a visa is granted. Chau called the pattern strange and not normal, while not ruling out sudden employer-side changes — though he said it is rare for firms to secure quotas and then leave them unused. Where a company wins many places and leaves large numbers idle, he said the Labour Department should take the initiative: such a situation is "absolutely not ideal" and the firm "must explain".
