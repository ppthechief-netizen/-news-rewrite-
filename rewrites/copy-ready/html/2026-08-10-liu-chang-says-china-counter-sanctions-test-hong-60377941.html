10th August 2026 - (Hong Kong) In a New Prospect for Hong Kong column, Dr Marco Liu Chang argues that within about ten days from late July into early August Beijing answered Europe and the United States with two sharply different counter-moves — matching 14 EU entities on export-control lists after Brussels tied 14 Chinese firms to Russia dual-use claims, then stacking measures against layered US curbs from the Federal Communications Commission through telecoms, drones, routers and subsea cables into advanced robots and power inverters — and that Hong Kong must decide whether it is merely squeezed or can sell trusted third-party compliance.

The Europe track, he writes, is spillover from Ukraine: the EU's 21st Russia package swept in Chinese entities as collateral for following Washington, and Beijing's sub-24-hour mirror list of 14 EU names answered in kind without theatrical excess. The US track is strategic rivalry as normal: after leaders met in Busan and even after 30th July virtual trade talks, Washington the next day listed more than 40 Chinese firms under the Uyghur Forced Labor Prevention Act, which he calls talking at the table while knifing behind it. China's reply became a system — tighter drone-component export controls toward the US, paused follow-up inspections at some US-invested plants, a US compliance-testing firm on counter-lists, a national-security probe of US-made printers and copiers, six US entities named — hitting supply chains, certification, market access and supervision where pressure landed.

Why precision with residual room instead of slogans? Since 2020, he notes, the Export Control Law, Unreliable Entity List rules and Anti-Foreign Sanctions Law turned counters into statute rather than temper. Gallium, germanium, rare earths and drone cores are nodes China can pinch without blanket bans, aiming costs at those who sanction while leaving off-ramps and exceptions so measures stay bargaining chips. Hong Kong appears on some EU sanction lists, he warns, so local trade and logistics already sit in European enforcement sights; as fights shift from "what is sold" to "who inspects and what counts as made in China", the city is both most exposed and potentially most useful for testing, certification and supply-chain due diligence — if both camps still trust its papers. The larger shift he celebrates is from enduring sanctions to lawful, multi-tool counters that still leave a path back to negotiation; for Hong Kong's political and business class, risk compliance plus openings from mainland industrial upgrading and anti-sanction architecture are the core homework of a permanent rules contest.
