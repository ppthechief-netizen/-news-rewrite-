10th August 2026 - (Hong Kong) In his New Prospect for Hong Kong column, Dr Marco Liu Chang writes that from late July into early August Beijing played two counter-sanction hands within about ten days — first listing 14 European Union entities on export-control lists, then targeting layered US curbs from the Federal Communications Commission through telecoms, drones, routers and subsea cables into advanced robots and power inverters — and asks whether Hong Kong is ready to be present in a contest that has shifted from slogans to statute and supply-chain nodes.

The Europe and US tracks arrived close together, he argues, but their trigger logics differ sharply. Sino-European friction is secondary spillover from the Russia-Ukraine war: the EU's 21st Russia sanctions package swept 14 Chinese entities onto dual-use restriction lists for alleged help to Russia's military industry — treating Chinese firms, in his view, as collateral for following Washington's Russia policy. Beijing's reply came in under 24 hours with matching export controls on 14 EU entities of like character, a one-for-one answer with tight measure. Sino-US friction, by contrast, is normalised collision under strategic rivalry. The afterglow of the Busan leaders' meeting had barely cooled when the FCC kept stacking limits across telecoms, drones, routers and subsea cables, then reaching toward advanced robotics and power inverters. More provocative still, on 30th July the US and Chinese trade leads had just finished a video call when Washington the next day listed more than 40 Chinese firms under the so-called Uyghur Forced Labor Prevention Act entity list — talking at the table, he writes, while knifing behind it.

Facing multi-line pressure, China's counters upgraded into a systemic combination: tighter US-bound export controls on key drone components; paused follow-up inspections at some US-invested factories in China; a US compliance-testing company placed on counter-lists; a national-security investigation into US-made printers, copiers and office equipment; and six US entities named directly — hitting supply chains, certification systems, market access and compliance supervision where American pressure had landed.

Why has Beijing stopped at verbal protest, and why are today's counters precise yet still leave room? First, he says, a legalised and institutionalised toolkit: Western sanctions often wore "national security" clothes with high arbitrariness, but since 2020 China has rolled out the Export Control Law, Unreliable Entity List rules and the Anti-Foreign Sanctions Law, so counters rest on statute rather than temper — rule-based bargaining that uses the West's own "rule-of-law language" to unpack hegemonic logic, which he calls a form of soft power. Second, precise grip on supply-chain nodes: gallium, germanium, rare earths and core drone parts are strategic goods where China holds global pricing power; Beijing has not banned trade wholesale, only pinched dual-use items and key nodes — precision guidance aimed at those who join sanctions or push restrictions, building real deterrence and real costs while limiting spillover into normal commerce and full escalation. The measures are also elastic, almost all carrying exceptions and adjustment space so enforcement can flex with the other side's attitude: counters are means, not ends — pressure tools and negotiating chips — with room to ease if the other side corrects course and further options if it escalates, keeping the initiative to advance or stand back.

For Hong Kong the EU sanction lists that already name local companies are not abstract great-power theatre: local trade and logistics firms sit inside European enforcement sights. The city's historic role is as a re-export and compliance hub. As the battlefield shifts from "what is sold" to "who inspects and what counts as made in China", Hong Kong is both the most directly squeezed party and potentially the one with the most opportunity — third-party testing, certification and supply-chain due diligence are businesses geopolitics enlarges — provided local institutions' credibility can still be accepted by both camps. That is both the hard problem and the assignment.

Looking back, he finds the impressive note not "fierceness" but "steadiness": from passively enduring foreign sanctions to skilfully combining legal, trade and technology tools for precise counters, China's economic diplomacy has crossed from "keeping a low profile" to "precise countering". The thread throughout remains plain: counters exist to bring counterparts back to the negotiating table; struggle is not the aim but a way to win the right to fair cooperation. In a volatile great-power contest, precision, restraint and residual room may be the most Chinese path of bargaining. For political and business circles in Hong Kong, how to run risk and compliance assessments inside this increasingly normalised contest of institutions and rules — and how to seize openings from mainland industrial upgrading and anti-sanction architecture — will decide who stays standing in the change.
