10th August 2026 - (Hong Kong) Last Sunday, 9th August, the Hong Kong Observatory headquarters logged a maximum of 36.9C — the highest since records began in 1884 — while Sheung Shui hit 39.8C, the territory's top automatic-station reading since those instruments arrived in the 1980s; yet the Labour Department's work heat stress warning stayed only at yellow, meaning open-air workers on moderate labour are merely advised 15 minutes' rest each hour, and moderate workers indoors without air-conditioning get no extra break at all — prompting former Observatory director Lam Chiu-ying to snap that cancelling the scheme "would make little difference".

By about 10.30am at King's Park the air temperature had already pierced 34C, but the Hong Kong Heat Index (HKHI) was still only 29; around 1.30pm the index finally reached 30 as temperatures neared 36C. The department had issued a yellow work heat warning that Sunday morning, urging employers to take proper steps against heat stroke, yet the trigger was not the index itself clearing the yellow threshold of 30 early — it still had not — but the Observatory's earlier "extreme heat" special tip forecasting daytime highs of 35C or above. Only because Labour, from 2024, linked work heat warnings to that extreme-heat tip did moderate outdoor crews gain rest advice before afternoon; without the hook, the same workers might have waited until air temperatures of 36C or more before any break guidance appeared.

HKHI was launched by the Observatory in 2014 with the Chinese University of Hong Kong Faculty of Medicine, adding public-hospital "excess admission rates" after concluding that classic wet-bulb globe temperature (WBGT) — rooted in North American military-camp climates and uniform conditions — may not fit Hong Kong's humid, low-wind, subtropical dense-city setting. Like WBGT it combines dry-bulb, wet-bulb and black-globe temperatures, but HKHI lifts wet-bulb weight from 0.7 to 0.8, dry-bulb from 0.1 to 0.15, and slashes black-globe from 0.2 to 0.05, so relative humidity weighs far more heavily. Under tropical-cyclone peripheral sinking air that brings high heat with low humidity, HKHI therefore rises only modestly; by contrast, on 12th August 2019 under south-westerlies, King's Park's mean relative humidity was 82 per cent and the index peaked at 32.4 — still HKHI's record. The difficulty, the editorial stresses, is that HKHI was designed for the general public who spend most time indoors; as Mr Lam notes, it is poorly matched to open-air work. Original research already showed excess hospital admissions rising markedly from about 29.5 and surging around 30.3, yet Labour's yellow band only begins at an index of 30 with a 15-minute hourly rest suggestion for outdoor moderate work — no wonder the scheme feels "out of touch".

In more than three years the warning has been issued some 200 times, yet red has never appeared. Red needs an index of 32 to 34; at roughly 50 per cent relative humidity that could mean air temperatures near 40C and the risk of more than a hundred ordinary residents seeking hospital care — and even then red guidance only advises 30 minutes' rest an hour for outdoor moderate labour or indoor heavy work without cooling. What kind of labour protection is that, the piece asks, when the guide is not legislated and has no direct statutory force? Employers still carry a general duty to keep workplaces safe, but how long staff may rest in extreme heat ultimately hinges on the boss, and arguing inside "so far as reasonably practicable" is hard.

Last June, then Federation of Trade Unions lawmaker Kwok Wai-keung asked Labour and Welfare Bureau Secretary Chris Sun Yuk-han whether thresholds would be lowered and whether site heat-stress risk assessments would be made mandatory by law. The secretary replied that if employers think the warning understates risk they "may make their own assessments", for example by measuring wet-bulb globe temperature — leaving the editorial to ask how many bosses the bureau truly expects to run WBGT science of their own, and why workers' health should rest on employers' voluntary willingness. Labour Department figures show about 20 to 30-plus heat-pressure work injuries a year over the past five years, with 31 cases from last year through February this year. As extreme weather worsens, civilian heat-stroke tragedies may multiply, let alone workplace illness; a citizen's heat stroke can be called "misfortune", but when a record bake still fails to trigger fuller rest and prevention because the work heat index sits "off the ground", misfortune is the wrong word. A people-centred administration, the editorial closes, sends the chief executive into the districts — bureau chiefs should also feel outdoor and non-air-conditioned workplaces in the heat, something no air-conditioned Tamar office can invent.
