10th August 2026 - (Hong Kong) Sunday's Observatory headquarters peak of 36.9C — the highest since records began in 1884 — and Sheung Shui's 39.8C, the top automatic-station reading since the 1980s, still left the Labour Department on a yellow work heat warning, meaning open-air moderate labour is only advised 15 minutes' rest an hour while moderate work in non-air-conditioned indoors gets no extra break at all, prompting former Observatory director Lam Chiu-ying to say the scheme might as well be scrapped.

King's Park crossed 34C by about 10.30am while the Hong Kong Heat Index stayed at 29, only reaching 30 near 1.30pm when air temperatures were already near 36C. Sunday's morning yellow alert leaned on the Observatory's extreme-heat special tip forecasting 35C or above — a 2024 linkage — rather than the index itself clearing 30 early; without that hook, moderate outdoor workers might have waited until afternoon for rest advice. HKHI, developed in 2014 with CUHK medicine to fold excess public-hospital admissions into a subtropical dense-city metric, weights wet-bulb more and black-globe far less than classic WBGT (0.05 versus 0.2), so dry sinking-air heat lifts it slowly while muggy days such as 12th August 2019, when King's Park humidity averaged 82 per cent, still hold the HKHI record at 32.4. Research behind the index already saw excess admissions rise sharply around 29.5–30.3, yet Labour's yellow band only starts at 30 with a 15-minute outdoor rest suggestion — and in more than three years and some 200 issuances, red (index 32–34) has never appeared, even though that band would still prescribe only 30 minutes' rest an hour for outdoor moderate or indoor heavy work, with no statutory force beyond general employer duties.

Last June then FTU lawmaker Kwok Wai-keung asked Labour and Welfare Bureau Secretary Chris Sun Yuk-han whether thresholds would be lowered and site heat assessments made mandatory; the reply was that employers who think the warning understates risk may assess wet-bulb globe temperature themselves — leaving workers' rest, critics say, to bosses' voluntary science. Official figures show about 20 to 30-plus heat-related work injuries a year over five years, 31 by February this year counting last year; as extremes worsen, the piece asks whether bureau chiefs should feel roadside and non-air-conditioned workplaces rather than judge heat from air-conditioned Tamar rooms.
