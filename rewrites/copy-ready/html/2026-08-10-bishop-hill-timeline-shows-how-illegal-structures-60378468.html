10th August 2026 - (Hong Kong) Illegal occupation of government land and unauthorised building on the slopes of Bishop Hill in Sham Shui Po — officially Woh Chai Shan — have dragged on for years without resolution; today, 10th August, the government announced a Woh Chai Shan enhancement plan that will clear unlawful and unsafe structures, facilities or clutter in two phases, prompting a rewind through roughly two decades of how informal exercise gear gave way to ever more builds.

Above Berwick Street in Shek Kip Mei, the hill neighbours usually call Bishop Hill was, for decades before the Romanesque summit service reservoir was recognised, a dawn-exercise ground, with the "sky ladder" to the top built by residents themselves. Why it grew into sports facilities is a longer story. HK01 reported earlier that Sars survivor So Chi-keung, around 2007 to 2008, found the reservoir plateau empty, cut the fencing wire, and added fitness equipment, stone tables and benches, a rain shelter and a swing to encourage neighbours to exercise, after which the spot became a gathering place. Sham Shui Po District Council was already discussing the site; 2009 papers of the district facilities committee show the Water Supplies Department saying that if another department applied to turn reservoir land into open space or recreation and took on later management, and if waterworks safety and operation were not harmed, WSD would cooperate accordingly, while the Leisure and Cultural Services Department said it had always supported better district facilities and would gladly join later management provided users followed LCSD recreation-ground rules.

By 2016, still before the heritage find, friction rose: WSD, then managing the summit reservoir open area, considered removing two large trees planted years earlier by morning walkers; residents objected and the dispute returned to the district council, with councillors calling the department "bureaucratic". Though neighbours had occupied the ground for years, WSD stressed Bishop Hill was not open for public use. More than a year later it explained that the reservoir structure had safety risks, with cracks on the roof, roots piercing into the interior and heavy tree growth adding load, so "the area is not suitable for public entry".

Late 2020 the hill's fate turned: the reservoir, then facing demolition, was revealed by gathering neighbours as a century-old Romanesque structure; WSD paused works and the reservoir was later graded a Grade 1 historic building. When HK01 reporters climbed at the time, residents had already shifted to vacant ground beside nearby footpaths, building temporary mahjong shelters from canvas and bamboo fences, and raising steel frames for outdoor fitness and table tennis — with no police or officials seen clearing them. After the reservoir was kept and WSD opened guided tours, the Home Affairs Department's Sham Shui Po District Management Committee in May 2022, citing rising footfall and complaints to departments, posted notices at seven spots on Bishop Hill requiring illegal items to be removed by mid-June, drawing more than 3,000 residents' signatures — including Mr So's — hoping to keep the facilities.

At the June 2022 full Sham Shui Po District Council meeting, then Association for Democracy and People's Livelihood councillors Tam Tak-ching and Li Ting-fung tabled a motion calling Bishop Hill a long-standing dawn-exercise haven, asking the government to keep the sports facilities, suspend demolition orders and plan a small park. Establishment voices joined: DAB Kowloon West lawmaker and Bishop Hill Concern Group convenor Vincent Cheng Wing-shun said that while conserving the reservoir the government should also revitalise hillside land and plan lawful facilities for neighbours, stressing that demolition without a substitute was not enough — there should be a replacement scheme. Then Sham Shui Po District Officer Paul Wong Yan-yin told the meeting that the Home Affairs Office planned extra seats at the hillside pavilion and a pebble path near the Berwick Street entrance, but emphasised those works were to provide facilities for residents, not as compensation for removing illegal structures, and that after illegal builds went, morning-walk path works would continue.

Those works, however, appear not to have proceeded as planned; years on, original structures were not cleared and instead multiplied. Late last year scaffolding rose at the Tong Yin Street hillside rest area on the mid-slope; Lee Lan-fong, calling herself chair of the "Bishop Hill Sports Association" and known as "Sister Fong", claimed a "Mao Zedong cultural hall" was being built. After the Lands Department cleared the site back to open ground, people again raised a "Hong Kong Mao Zedong Cultural Hall" with a large Mao statue; Lands recently returned to clear and issued another clearance order requiring the statue's removal by 9th February. Asked why the 2022 clearance order was not executed, Lands replied that the Sham Shui Po District Management Committee had planned to clear and remove various Bishop Hill facilities and structures in June 2022 but met strong resident opposition, so the committee deferred action — yet for new illegal occupations of government land after June 2022, the Lands Office would keep taking control measures. In the past three months it had patrolled Bishop Hill 33 times and repeatedly acted against newly built illegal occupations. Early this year Mr Cheng told HK01 he believed the authorities' "red line" against new builds on Bishop Hill was a "balanced approach": earlier facilities were mostly health-and-fitness gear, but later some people placed deities and even built temples. He still stressed real local demand for sports facilities and urged that once land is recovered in the long run the government should actively consider lawful, safe sports kit on the original site — such as table-tennis tables — to meet residents' needs.
