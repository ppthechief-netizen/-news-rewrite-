11th August 2026 - (Hong Kong) A non-statutory Work Heat Warning meant to protect outdoor workers has been raised more than two hundred times in just over three years, yet every alert has stayed at the lowest yellow level without a single red or black issuance, even as Sunday brought record-breaking temperatures.

Former Observatory director Lam Chiu-ying and wider public commentary have questioned whether the mechanism reflects real heat stress on labourers, with some arguing that warnings stuck at yellow offer little practical signal. Mr. HUI Chark Shum, Sam, JP, Commissioner for Labour, addressed the imbalance on RTHK's Millennium programme today, saying colleagues monitoring the recent spike likewise saw the level fail to jump. "We also have many question marks internally," he said.

Mr Hui explained that the Labour Department overhauled trigger rules in April, folding in additional Observatory station data and expecting a greater likelihood of higher-tier warnings this summer. The outcome, he conceded, diverged from that forecast. After the extreme heat episode, officials immediately inspected backend readings to learn whether the gap stemmed from isolated data quirks or structural design flaws.

Speaking on Commercial Radio's On a Clear Day programme, he said the department had assumed sensitivity would rise under the new arrangement, "but the actual effect still had a gap, so we must keep adjusting with climate change." Pressed on whether the past two days of record heat should have warranted at least a red warning under the revised framework, Mr Hui replied that it was "possible," while stressing that outcome remains a review target rather than a settled standard and that precise indicators still need re-examination.

On timing, he said he hoped to move quickly, ideally completing work this summer or before the current school holiday period ends, so future heatwaves trigger alerts that better match conditions workers face on the ground. The admission underscores pressure on a warning system that carries guidance but no direct legal force, leaving employers to interpret rest rules while the public watches thermometer readings climb far above the levels that have so far produced only yellow signals.
