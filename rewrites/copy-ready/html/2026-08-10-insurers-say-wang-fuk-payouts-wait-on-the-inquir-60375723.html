10th August 2026 - (Hong Kong) After the Wang Fuk Court fire, residents fixated on a HK$2 billion owners' corporation "fire" policy — formally building structural cover — and whether payouts die if the government refuses on-site rebuilding or buys claim rights with the titles; Hong Kong Federation of Insurers chief executive Selina Lau, without commenting on the specific contract disclosed earlier, walked through how such policies work, why the inquiry must come first, and how buyouts, public liability and home contents sit beside that shell cover.

Structural fire insurance protects the flat "shell" as handed over — walls, ceilings, floors, doors and windows — and the Wang Fuk policy, bought by the corporation through the management office, also blankets common parts across some 2,000 flats including corridors, rear stairs, lift shafts and pipework. It can respond to typhoon facade damage as well as fire. Dividing HK$2 billion by household into HK$1 million each is a myth, Ms Lau said: the product restores units toward their pre-loss, roughly 50-year-old Home Ownership Scheme condition rather than cashing out paper losses. If flats will not be reinstated as before — the government has ruled out rebuilding on site and is acquiring titles with claim rights attached — insurers must renegotiate valuation with the owner by estimating what reinstatement would have cost, which is one reason meaningful structural claims wait on the independent committee. Policy wording can allow reinstatement at another location in principle, but the practical post-buyout path remains under discussion.

Investigators have already aired flammable foam, non-flame-retardant scaffolding nets and allegedly disabled fire mains and alarms during major works. Ms Lau said payouts need the inquiry — and possibly later criminal probes — to pin down cause and whether works breached statutory-compliance warranties such as Fire Services or Labour Department rules, because human or non-compliant factors can shrink or reshape indemnity. She understands owners who feel claim rights are being "gifted" to the government, yet argues a buyer of unrepaired fire-damaged flats reasonably takes the insurance with them — and that buyout prices already partly bank expected recoveries, pre-paying disaster victims and sparing them a Grenfell Tower-style slog that only recently closed in London even under single ownership. Unburnt Wang Chi House still complicates how much of the estate pot government and insurers must apportion. Separate personal fire policies share reinstatement under a once-only principle rather than paying twice; how those sit beside the corporation rights the government is taking is still unsettled. Statutory public liability — at least HK$10 million, topped to HK$20 million — and about HK$50 million of contractors' all-risks may help third parties such as cars crushed by scaffolding once main and sub-contractor fault is sorted, another long legal road. Home contents insurers have already paid above HK$500 million by early February under special no-receipt arrangements, clearing nearly nine-tenths of affected cases at limits often HK$800,000 to HK$1.2 million, plus HK$80,000 to HK$100,000 alternative-accommodation stipends and even spoiled freezer food. Looking ahead, Ms Lau expects tighter independent checks on fire alarms when estates buy cover — premiums may rise where overnight total loss of more than a thousand flats looks plausible, or fall if management proves vigilant — and urges households to tell trusted relatives their policy numbers before the next crisis.
