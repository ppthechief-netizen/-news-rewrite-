11th August 2026 - (Hong Kong) Claiming workers' compensation after a heatstroke death remains extremely difficult under Hong Kong rules that demand a sole causal link with work, advocates say, as yellow Work Heat Warnings continue through extreme heat that pushed Sheung Shui to 39.8C on 9th August — the highest automatic-station reading since the 1980s.

Many outdoor workers have kept labouring under the Labour Department's yellow alerts. A mid-year Hong Kong Federation of Trade Unions survey of more than 500 workers found over half reported dizziness, headache or other heatstroke symptoms after inadequate rest, with some needing hospital care. The department told media that if an employee is injured or killed by an accident arising out of and in the course of employment — including heat-stress accidents — the employer must meet compensation duties, but whether any case counts as an occupational injury turns on the facts and cannot be generalised.

Secretary for Labour and Welfare Mr. SUN Yuk Han, Chris, JP told legislators in a written reply last year that from 2022 to 2024 there were 76 heatstroke or heat-stress related occupational injury cases, including one death. Those figures show heat harm is already entering the official injury ledger, yet fatal claims still face a high legal bar.

Fay Siu Sin-man, director of the Association for the Rights of Industrial Accident Victims, told Commercial Radio's On a Clear Day that heatstroke death claims are hard to win because families must prove a "sole relationship" between the cause of death and work. If an autopsy finds heart or vascular disease, the death is often treated as personal illness rather than an occupational injury. Some families commission scarce expert reports to show work caused the death, yet ARIAV has for years been unable to prove any case as a heat-related occupational fatality. Elsewhere, she noted, proof may require only that work contributed about half of the harm, a less exacting test than Hong Kong's sole-cause approach.

Federation of Trade Unions legislator the Honourable Ken LEE Kwong-yu said heatstroke is not clearly defined as an occupational injury under present labour law, making claims harder. He urged the Labour Department to study bringing outdoor work heatstroke into the injury framework and, drawing on the travel industry's levy-backed compensation fund model, to create a dedicated fund financed by a payroll deduction or employer payment so injured workers can receive support when the ordinance fails to cover them. He said he would discuss the idea with the department the following day.

Together the union survey, the three-year injury tally and the advocates' courtroom experience paint a gap between yellow warnings that urge rest and a compensation system that still struggles to recognise fatal heat as work harm. For families of outdoor workers the practical question is whether proof rules, expert scarcity and undefined statutory status will keep heat deaths outside the injury regime even as temperatures set new records.
