10th August 2026 - (Hong Kong) Whenever the Legislative Council recesses for summer, debate returns over whether members should enjoy a "summer holiday", and in recent months some lawmakers privately complained that being told to stay in Hong Kong for meetings had wrecked travel plans — reigniting the argument. In an exclusive interview, veteran Business and Professionals Alliance member Prof Hon Priscilla Leung Mei-fun said members must put the public first and that the idea of taking summer rest should not appear in a legislator's dictionary.

She urged colleagues to rethink what the role of a LegCo member demands.

The chamber has long kept a summer recess so many representatives can work their districts more deeply or spend time with family. This year, however, because the Northern Metropolis dedicated bill and other legislation need handling, some members were told not to travel abroad and to stay ready for meetings in Hong Kong. Some privately grumbled that the arrangement ruined plans; others said they did not want to sacrifice family trips and might therefore skip joining bills committees — remarks that drew wide public attention.

As chairwoman of the Northern Metropolis Bills Committee, Ms Leung said that in eighteen years as a legislator she has never treated summer as a holiday period and instead uses the recess for district work and to prepare for the new session. Serving as a member, she said, means putting residents first and reaching a selfless, almost egoless, stance. In earlier years of confrontational sittings with the opposition, she recalled, there was no talk of holidays and even daily life felt constrained.

She noted that during the 2023 recess LegCo still scrutinised the Chinese University of Hong Kong (Amendment) Bill, and nobody then asked why the council had to sit — the question was not even raised. She understands that everyone wants to be with family and that accompanying relatives when time allows is natural, but public business must come first. Colleagues, she repeated, need to straighten out their sense of the member's role.

To critics who say the quality of the legislature fell after electoral improvements, Ms Leung said she believes those who enter LegCo are capable people, though backgrounds differ and some have thinner political seasoning. Getting into the council is not simple, she stressed; newcomers can spend more time in the districts to grasp public sentiment and residents' expectations.

Earlier, unnamed officials called some members' remarks alarmist or said their votes barely mattered, and a department issued a rebuttal accusing members of rumour and misleading statements, prompting worry that "executive-led" government might harden into "executive high-handedness". Ms Leung declined to referee who was right or wrong, but said that under an executive-led system the government and LegCo stand as equals and officials also enjoy free speech — officials may rebut members and members may rebut them back, so nobody need take offence.

A member's vocation, she said, is to act as society's thermometer and to speak up actively, because one may be the only member voicing that point. The essential test is that remarks have substance and are not showmanship. Supporting executive-led government, she added, includes offering officials good-faith, constructive proposals. She hopes both the executive and the legislature give each other space and trust that the other side's views are offered with good intent.
