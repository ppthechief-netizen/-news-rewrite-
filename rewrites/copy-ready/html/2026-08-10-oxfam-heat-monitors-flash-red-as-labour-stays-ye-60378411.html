10th August 2026 - (Hong Kong) On Sunday, 9th August, when the Observatory logged a record 36.9C, the Labour Department held a yellow work heat warning all day and never stepped up to red or black — drawing former Observatory director Lam Chiu-ying to call the system "out of touch" for under-weighting sun exposure on outdoor workers and to urge internationally common wet-bulb globe temperature (WBGT), which weights solar radiation more heavily; since the department launched work heat warnings in May 2023, red and black have never been issued.

In 2024 Oxfam designed a heat-prevention alert system with Cyberport health-tech firm LinkAIQ Limited, built from three parts: WBGT monitoring instruments, a network platform and smart watches. The meters measure wet-bulb globe temperature — combining solar and nearby radiant heat, air temperature and relative humidity into an internationally used heat-stress index — and are easy to site by roadsides or near workers' actual posts, with wind and rain resistance. Singapore and Japan issue heat-stroke alerts on WBGT and install instruments on large construction sites. Oxfam's platform continuously receives station readings and issues district-level heat alerts; workers' watches, with built-in network cards, can sync both Observatory/Labour warnings and the local monitoring alerts, while also reading heart rate and skin temperature so staff can track their bodies in real time and raise heat-prevention awareness.

The Observatory heat stress index, by a set formula, gives black-globe temperature (solar radiation) only about 5 per cent weight and natural wet-bulb factors (humidity and wind) about 80 per cent. Oxfam instead uses WBGT, a common international occupational-safety heat-stress standard with heavier regard for solar radiation. Mr Lam says it is "completely inappropriate" for Labour to take the Observatory index straight as the warning standard because that index targets the general public, not outdoor workers, and fails to reflect prolonged sun exposure. Official warnings lean mainly on the King's Park station; Oxfam plants meters beside cleaners' and construction environments — pedestrian roadsides, site perimeters — measuring the heat stress where people actually stand.

From 23rd August to 10th September last year — 19 days — Oxfam, with the Association for the Rights of Industrial Accident Victims, the Hong Kong Catholic Commission for Labour Affairs and other groups, studied outdoor workers' heat stress and published findings in January. Monitors sat at seven places — Yau Ma Tei, Kwun Tong, Aberdeen, Kennedy Town, Tsuen Wan, Kwai Chung and Tai Wo Hau Road — near cleaning or construction settings, converting local readings into the department's colour bands for comparison with official warnings.

Across those 19 days Labour issued yellow on only four days, but the seven Oxfam stations averaged 14 yellow days — about 3.5 times Labour's count. Mean alert hours reached 95.23, more than triple the official tally; Tsuen Wan alone logged 140 hours, more than 110 hours above Labour's roughly 30 hours. Labour never left yellow, while every monitoring station issued red and even black. The worst site was Kwun Tong, with six black days and 11 red days; Kennedy Town saw three black and 13 red days. Even within the same broad area — King's Park versus Yau Ma Tei, or Kwai Chung versus Tai Wo Hau Road — heat stress diverged, showing that reliance on a single reference station can underestimate risk elsewhere.

Work heat warnings themselves carry no direct compulsory legal force and only advise employers on rest or stop-work standards; yet if a firm fails to take proper heat prevention and rest measures and an industrial accident follows, that may breach the Occupational Safety and Health Ordinance. The same study interviewed 64 outdoor cleaners: 94 per cent had shown at least one early heat-illness symptom in extreme heat; about 75 per cent said companies did not adjust working hours; more than 35 per cent lacked enough drinking water; nearly 40 per cent said firms had no special arrangements tied to heat warnings; nearly 70 per cent received waist fans, but workers said stock was issued only once, batteries drained fast, units were not waterproof and follow-up support was missing; and nearly 97 per cent said employers never ran a separate on-site heat-stress assessment — a clear gap between paper guidance and roadside reality.
