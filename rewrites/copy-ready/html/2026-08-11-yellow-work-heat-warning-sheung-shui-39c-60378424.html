11th August 2026 - (Hong Kong) The Labour Department held a territory-wide yellow Work Heat Warning yesterday as sinking air on the fringe of tropical cyclone White Dolphin drove extreme heat, with Sheung Shui peaking at 39.3C even though that district's heat stress index stayed below the yellow trigger.

Observatory readings at 4.50pm yesterday showed several stations at 37C or above: Sheung Shui 39.3C, Wong Tai Sin 38.1C and Yuen Long 38C, while Sha Tin, Tai Po, Tsuen Wan and other districts reached 37C. Heat stress indices elsewhere mostly sat between 28 and 30, with Wong Chuk Hang above 30. Sheung Shui, despite posting the highest air temperature, recorded a heat stress index of only 28.2 — short of the Labour Department's yellow warning mark of 30.

The department nonetheless issued the yellow warning at 7.00am and kept it in force until 6.00pm. Officials stress that the Work Heat Warning is a territory-wide alert, not a district-by-district call based on whichever automatic station happens to run hottest. Employers are required, while a warning is in force, to follow the Guide on Preventing Heat Stroke at Work and arrange rest breaks and other heat-protection measures for staff.

Under the present bands, yellow applies when the heat stress index reaches 30 but stays below 32; red covers 32 to below 34; and black is issued at 34 or above. Former Observatory director Lam Chiu-ying has criticised the department for anchoring worker warnings to the Observatory heat stress index, arguing the measure was designed for the general public rather than outdoor labourers and under-weights solar radiation.

According to the Observatory website, Hong Kong's heat stress index blends dry-bulb temperature, natural wet-bulb temperature and black-globe temperature. Dry-bulb temperature — the everyday air reading — carries a 15 per cent weighting. Natural wet-bulb temperature, which reflects sweating and cooling outdoors, accounts for 80 per cent. Black-globe temperature, which captures solar radiation and wind effects, is weighted at only 5 per cent.

An Observatory blog note has observed that on sunny, weakly winded days the black-globe reading can run more than ten degrees above the dry-bulb figure. Lam has urged a switch toward wet-bulb globe temperature style metrics that give solar load a heavier share, saying the current mix fails to describe workers under prolonged sun exposure.

Yesterday's contrast — record-high district air temperatures alongside heat indices that never cleared the yellow district threshold in Sheung Shui — has sharpened that debate. Outdoor crews still faced a full day of yellow-level precautions under the territory-wide alert, even as the single hottest neighbourhood's index sat nearly two points short of 30.

For site managers the practical message remained unchanged while yellow was in force: schedule rest, provide water and shade, and treat the warning as a citywide duty rather than a local thermometer race. Whether the next revision of the scheme will move closer to black-globe or wet-bulb globe temperature weighting is a policy question Lam and other critics continue to press.
