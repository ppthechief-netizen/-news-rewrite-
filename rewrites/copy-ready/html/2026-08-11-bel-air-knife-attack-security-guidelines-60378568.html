11th August 2026 - (Hong Kong) Security industry leaders have praised Bel-Air guards who confronted a knife-wielding man yesterday morning, saying they kept a safe distance, used improvised tools and calmed the attacker while police made an on-the-spot arrest after a domestic helper, a male security officer and a cat were injured.

Online clips show the suspect chasing a guard with a blade; when one officer fell, a colleague used hand signals to soothe the man and talk him back. Separate footage captured a foreign domestic helper collapsing after being stabbed in the back as the attacker closed in, only for an African man to kick the assailant aside while another guard interposed a broom and broke the assault.

Chamber of Security Industry chairman Johnny Ho Kai-man said frontline staff had already done their utmost without special weapons, calling a stick "the safest method" to divert an attacker's attention. Industry guidelines, he said, require guards who meet a knife-holder to radio the control room and call police first, keep a safe distance, avoid rushing in, try to calm the person, summon more colleagues and, within a safe zone, use nearby objects for defence or distraction.

Ho added that in this case the emergency was so acute that officers stepped forward to protect residents — an exceptional response that should be commended rather than condemned. Some estates may keep protective shields, he noted, but they are rarely reachable in the first seconds of a sudden attack. Hong Kong security staff do not carry offensive gear such as pitchforks or batons, which police tightly regulate; if seized by an attacker they could endanger the neighbourhood, and dense urban policing usually brings officers to the scene quickly. Body-worn cameras are now common and are meant to deter, he said, because being filmed makes many suspects pause.

Since the Diamond Hill Plaza Hollywood killing, the trade has stepped up crisis-intervention training — teaching verbal, tone and body-language cues to spot unstable people early, using an "interview stance" and distance rules to build trust while keeping teams safe. Ho also urged management companies and owners' corporations to rethink staffing: many estates have cut numbers for cost reasons and can handle routine patrols, yet two or three officers struggle when a serious incident erupts.

Further clips circulating on Threads and provided by readers show a bare-chested, barefoot man with a knife sprinting uphill toward passers-by, cornering a sunglass-wearing estate guard at a car-park entrance, raising the blade as the fallen officer kicked to keep space, then pressing the chase into the roadway as colleagues shouted him down and waved minibuses and foreign pedestrians away. In one sequence the attacker ignored a shouted "Don't move!" and stabbed a woman in the back as she fled across the road; when she fell, the African man — later said by sources to be a friend of the knife-wielder — kicked the assailant clear while a broom-wielding guard held him off.

The overlapping videos have become the public record of how quickly a residential slope turned into a running confrontation, and of how lightly equipped guards improvised distance, voice and household tools until police arrived. For the industry the lesson is twofold: follow the safe-distance playbook when possible, and treat genuine life-saving exceptions as acts of courage rather than breaches of protocol.
