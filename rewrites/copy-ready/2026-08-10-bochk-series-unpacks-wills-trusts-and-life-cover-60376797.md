10th August 2026 - (Hong Kong) As wealth succession becomes a discipline that needs early planning, Bank of China (Hong Kong) Private Wealth has launched Think Tank · Succession, a four-part dialogue series hosted by veteran financial broadcaster Karina Fan, inviting different experts each episode to unpack wealth management and how assets endure across generations before a family crisis forces rushed decisions.

Solicitor Tang Chi Wing stresses that many Hong Kong people still believe a "joint forever" arrangement or a joint account makes a will unnecessary. Succession, he says, is a legal process that cannot be treated lightly: assets in a deceased person's name — bank accounts, property, shares or MPF — generally need probate, and informal handling can amount to unlawful disposal. He also rejects the idea that trusts are only for the ultra-rich sitting on more than HK$100 million; the point is protecting dependants, so anyone with a flat or savings who cares about their family's future life should learn the toolkit early and spare relatives later grief.

Wills under the Wills Ordinance, the experts note, are the most direct route — simple, flexible and relatively cheap for clear families and straightforward estates. Yet a will is a one-shot arrangement that takes effect only on death: it cannot manage assets or control risk while the settlor is alive, may be void if statutory formalities fail, and even a valid will can see part of an estate reallocated to certain dependants under the law if the rules on provision are breached.

Trusts Mr Tang likens to a multi-function remote — settlors can set investment style, payout timing and beneficiaries across generations, often sidestepping probate delay and shielding assets from marital or debt claims against family members. That continuity comes at a price: heavier legal architecture and higher set-up and ongoing running fees than a simple will.

BOC Life's Ngai Chi-wai adds that people planning wealth increasingly want family financial protection and succession as well as their own needs, which is where life cover enters. Modern products can blend death benefit — often pure term cover — with savings, investment and succession aims. Approved claims, once required papers clear under each insurer's rules, can pay named beneficiaries directly and help with living costs without waiting on probate — a practical edge when cash is needed quickly.

Market products may allow a change of insured life or a backup insured once proof and insurer approval are in place under policy terms; owners can also stage when and how much beneficiaries receive according to their wishes for the next generation. Policyholders still carry insurer credit risk, and early termination or surrender can return far less than premiums paid — points repeated in the programme's risk warnings and sales-document caveats. The materials stress the content is general reference only, not professional advice, solicitation or an offer to sell products outside jurisdictions where distribution would break the law.

Family backgrounds, Mr Tang says from cross-boundary Hong Kong-mainland cases, are often more tangled than people expect: the biggest risk is rarely sibling greed but incomplete records and unclear assets — remarried households, half-siblings, or overseas holdings with no paper trail — disputes that can drag for years and burn money and time. Mr Ngai frames insurance as one tile in a wider life-wealth puzzle, not the end of planning; generations seldom talk first, so professionals usually surface each side's financial needs and succession vision, then match tools to personal finances, fees and product risk before choosing a life product for a complete plan.

The series does not speak for Bank of China (Hong Kong) or BOC Life, which accept no liability for the views aired, and again warns that early surrender can leave holders far short of premiums paid. Viewers are told to read policy terms and sales documents carefully and to treat the dialogues as education rather than personal advice.
