10th August 2026 - (Hong Kong) Peripheral sinking airflow from Super Typhoon White Dolphin left Hong Kong in extreme heat yesterday (9th August), the hottest day in nearly 143 years of records since 1884: the Observatory headquarters logged a record 36.9C, while Sheung Shui soared to 39.8C around 3.20pm, the highest since automatic weather stations were installed in the 1980s — yet the Labour Department's work heat warning stayed at the lowest yellow tier.

Former Observatory director Lam Chiu-ying has said bluntly that the warning thresholds are so high the scheme has become a display piece. Today (10th August) Fay Siu Sin-man, director of the Association for the Rights of Industrial Accident Victims, also posted that the authorities should consider a dual-track — even multi-track — approach to data indicators so yellow, red or black can be issued more quickly.

Ms Siu said this morning that the public is debating how the Labour Department's Guide on Preventing Heat Stroke at Work left yesterday's extremes still on the lowest yellow warning. Several former Observatory directors, she noted, endorse the wet-bulb globe temperature index used widely around the world, and Oxfam has long collected and organised such figures. Last year, compared with the Labour Department's heat stress index, the wet-bulb globe readings reached red on several days when the department's measure never did.

Officials have replied that today's heat stress index already folds in humidity and same-day Accident and Emergency attendances and better fits Hong Kong. There has even been a day when the air temperature was only 30C yet high humidity still brought a yellow heat warning — a case where the present mechanism seemed ahead of a pure wet-bulb globe reading. Even so, Ms Siu argues the government could run dual or multiple tracks: whichever mechanism's figures first hit the standard for yellow, red or black should drive that colour, ending the quarrel over a single chosen index.

She added that getting heat stroke certified as a work injury for compensation remains extremely difficult, especially after a death. Labour Department figures show about 20 to 30 occupational heat-stroke injury cases a year in recent years, but zero recognised deaths. ARIAV has followed sudden deaths of workers on duty under yellow heat warnings that were ultimately treated as heart problems — personal illness outside the Employees' Compensation Ordinance — and such outcomes are not isolated. Families seeking justice and fair compensation face a crushing path: they must endure consenting to or seeking an autopsy after a sudden death; then find experts who will positively link the death to a hot working environment; then endure long litigation that, without Legal Aid funding, can impose huge costs. Almost all three steps are indispensable. Unless the present work-injury test of a single causal link — that the injury or death was caused wholly and directly by work, excluding every personal disease factor — can be broken open, she said, treating heat stroke as a work injury remains hollow and workers' families get no real protection.
