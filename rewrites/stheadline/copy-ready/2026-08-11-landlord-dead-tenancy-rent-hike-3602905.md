11th August 2026 - (Hong Kong) A male tenant posted WhatsApp screenshots on Threads claiming his landlord tried to lift monthly rent from $36,000 to $48,000 — a jump of about 33 per cent — while a two-year fixed, or "dead", tenancy was still in force.

Under a dead term both sides are bound for the agreed period: neither may walk away without cause, and rent is ordinarily frozen. The tenant said the mainland landlord of a Hong Kong flat messaged a blunt new figure for the first of the following month: "48K", rendered in broken Chinese asking for forty-eight thousand "住金".

He replied that the lease was a two-year dead term with no mid-term increase or cancellation allowed, and that the landlord himself had demanded that structure when the papers were signed. The owner then asked whether two years had already passed and, in a voice-to-text slip that went viral, told the tenant to "wipe" — apparently meaning "check" — the documents for him.

The poster argued the wider pricing push may be fuelled by agents. On a major listing site, he said, the landlord's flats were advertised at about 30 per cent above market. He claimed another household had already been told to leave and that a fresh ask of $85,000 sat $27,000 above a prior figure. Even if the unit later let at the old rent, he suggested, agents could earn commission again — making them the real winners. He added that the owner bought ten flats four years ago, including five on the same floor as his.

Readers tore into the disregard for contract. Some joked the lease was treated as a "historical document", useful only when it favoured the owner. Others mocked the idea of paying tenants being drafted as unpaid clerks to "wipe" the paperwork.

Practical advice poured in. Several urged the tenant, when moving out, to withhold the last two months' rent against the deposit lest every scuff be used to claw money back. Others suggested checking stamp duty and tax filings; the poster later said the landlord had stamped the lease properly and was "safer" than him on that point.

The tenant said he would not yield while the dead term runs, and that the landlord went quiet after the pushback. Commenters still warned him to hunt for a new flat before the remaining year ends, expecting another hard rise or forced exit once the freeze lifts. The episode has become a short primer on why fixed terms matter in a tight rental market — and how screenshots of a botched rent demand can turn a private dispute into citywide ridicule.
