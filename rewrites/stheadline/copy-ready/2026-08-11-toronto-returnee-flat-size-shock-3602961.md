11th August 2026 - (Hong Kong) A Hongkonger who once emigrated to Toronto and has since returned says the jump from a cramped public flat to a multi-thousand-square-foot Canadian house left him wondering how anyone lives in Hong Kong's tight spaces again.

In a Threads post that later circulated widely, he traced a ladder of floor area that began long before Canada. Before finishing university he shared a public-housing flat of about 200-plus square feet with family. He later rented roughly 500 square feet in an older private block and, for the first time, had a room of his own.

After moving to Toronto he first rented about 700 square feet and realised the master suite alone already outstripped his former public flat. He later bought a detached house of about 2,000 square feet with a further 1,000-square-foot basement. The leap, he wrote, made the pursuit of floor area feel endless.

Space changed how he judged past homes. Once inside the Toronto house he said earlier Hong Kong dwellings suddenly felt unfit for living. Visits to an employer's and a colleague's parents' properties reinforced the contrast: one home, he claimed, gave even a dog room its own bathroom larger than his master suite, while another could park more than ten cars at once.

He also relayed a familiar caution that many people later want to downsize because a huge house becomes a burden, not a prize. That line opened the door for readers who have lived the other side of the dream.

Online replies focused on labour, ageing and services rather than square footage alone. Some said two people can manage with about 900 square feet if the layout is practical, naming wishes such as a separate laundry room and clear dry-wet zoning. Others warned that weak knees and backs make multi-storey houses a daily chore in later life, forcing moves downstairs and undoing the point of a grand footprint.

One commenter living in an 8,000-square-foot garden house said a 500-square-foot flat with domestic help felt happier than solo upkeep of a mansion. Another, raised in Europe with little affection for large homes, said after returning to Hong Kong they preferred a tiny flat of 100 to 200 square feet provided it offered room service and round-the-clock security for peace of mind. A further folk note warned that people should not occupy homes so large they cannot "hold them down".

The exchange has revived a familiar Hong Kong argument: floor area is scarce and emotionally charged, yet comfort may depend as much on layout, help and safety as on raw square footage. For returnees who have tasted Canadian roominess, re-adapting to local flats can feel abrupt; for locals reading the thread, the Toronto house reads less like an unambiguous upgrade and more like a trade-off with cleaning, stairs and cost attached.
